# Findings

Checked against the ABS and RBA extracts downloaded on 12 November 2026. Living-cost and price figures run to the September quarter 2026. The wage result runs to the June quarter 2026. The September wage release is due on 18 November 2026 and is not in the extract. Annual price changes are calculated from the published price levels, because that percentage-change series was not returned by the API.

## Working households are behind on both horizons

Over the year to September 2026, living costs for employee households rose **5.1%**. Prices across the eight capitals rose **3.9%** (3.92% before rounding). The latest wage result, total hourly rates excluding bonuses for the private and public sectors together, rose **3.2%** over the year to June 2026.

In that June quarter, the last one where pay and living costs can be compared, employee living costs rose **3.7%**. Pay was **0.5 percentage points** behind living costs and **0.7 percentage points** behind prices (3.94% before rounding).

From September 2021 to June 2026 the gap is much larger. Employee living costs rose **28.9%**. Wages rose **17.6%**. Prices rose **23.0%**. A basket that cost working households $100 in September 2021 cost about $129 by June 2026, while wages that started at $100 reached about $118.

By September 2026, employee living costs were **31.4%** above September 2021, against a **24.6%** rise in prices.

## Mortgage interest is back in the employee basket

Interest charges were **14.9%** of the employee budget at the December 2024 weight update, the latest the API publishes. In September 2022 that share was **6.2%**. Age-pensioner households were at **1.8%**, and self-funded retirees at **1.2%**. The separate mortgage-interest weight is not in the extract, so the budget share is interest charges as a group. The price series below is mortgage interest itself.

Mortgage interest for employee households rose **12.9%** over the year to September 2026, after falling through late 2025. In the September quarter the employee living-cost level rose **2.00 points**, and mortgage interest contributed **0.79 points**, about two-fifths of that quarter's rise.

The cash-rate target is **4.85%**, set on 4 November 2026. It was **3.60%** on 13 August 2025. It was raised five times in 2026, by 0.25 percentage points each time (4 February, 18 March, 6 May, 30 September, 4 November).

## Who is behind depends on the window

Over the year to September 2026:

| Household | Living costs | Versus prices (3.9%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Employee | 5.1% | +1.2 points |
| Other government payments | 4.9% | +1.0 point |
| Pensioner and beneficiary benchmark | 4.6% | +0.7 points |
| Age pensioner | 4.3% | +0.4 points |
| Self-funded retiree | 3.8% | −0.1 points |

Self-funded retirees are the only group whose living costs rose more slowly than prices. Age-pensioner costs (4.3%) rose more slowly than the pensioner and beneficiary benchmark (4.6%). Households on other government payments (4.9%) rose a full percentage point faster than prices.

From September 2021 to September 2026 the order is the same at the top: employees **31.4%**, other payment households **28.1%**, the pensioner benchmark **26.8%**, age pensioners **25.8%**, prices **24.6%**, self-funded retirees **23.3%**. The chart on the site stops the comparison in June 2026, at employees **28.9%**, prices **23.0%** and wages **17.6%**, so pay can be included.

Private-sector wages rose **3.1%** over the year to June and public-sector wages **3.4%**, against the combined **3.2%**.

Agriculture is not in this wage extract.

In September 2026, **4.7%** of the Australian workforce was looking for work (4.72% before rounding to one decimal). Victoria was **5.1%** and Tasmania **5.0%**. Seasonally adjusted rates for the Northern Territory and the ACT were not in the extract.

## The next print, with the range from past misses

The estimate uses whichever of a seasonal repeat and an automatic ARIMA had the smaller error on annual change over the last 20 quarters. For every series here, that was the automatic ARIMA. The wider range is the conformal interval built from those same past misses.

| Series | Quarter being estimated | Estimate | Wider range | Typical miss | Wider range contained the result |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Wages | September 2026, due 18 November | 3.2% | 2.9% to 3.6% | 0.2 points | 16 of 20 quarters |
| Prices | December 2026 | 4.3% | 3.1% to 5.4% | 0.6 points | 20 of 20 |
| Employee households | December 2026 | 6.4% | 4.8% to 8.0% | 0.7 points | 20 of 20 |
| Other payment households | December 2026 | 5.7% | 4.3% to 7.1% | 0.8 points | 18 of 20 |
| Pensioner benchmark | December 2026 | 5.3% | 3.8% to 6.8% | 0.7 points | 18 of 20 |
| Age pensioners | December 2026 | 5.0% | 3.4% to 6.6% | 0.7 points | 17 of 20 |
| Self-funded retirees | December 2026 | 4.0% | 2.9% to 5.1% | 0.6 points | 18 of 20 |

The wage range is narrow and still sits around the June result of 3.2%. The living-cost ranges are wide. A band that contained the last 20 employee results runs from 4.8% to 8.0%, so the December figure can move a long way and still be inside it. That is the uncertainty, not a precise call.

If September wages land near 3.2% while employee living costs are already 5.1% and the December estimate is 6.4%, pay does not catch up in this round of figures.
